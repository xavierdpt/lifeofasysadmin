# Exit 137 at Twenty Minutes

*Theme: The one-character bug*

`bench03` is the shared compute box in the corner of the second floor — twelve
cores, no scheduler, whoever gets there first — and it has been running the auto
nice daemon since before Priya inherited it. That is what `and` is for: it wakes
up every sixty seconds, walks every process on the machine, adds up the CPU time
each one has burned, and looks the process up in `/etc/and.priorities` to decide
what its nice level ought to be by now. Short things are left alone. Long things
sink. It is a policy engine for the one question nobody wants to be paged about,
which is who gets the core when two people want it.

The photogrammetry group's solver went into that file in the spring, after a
fortnight of complaints that interactive work on `bench03` had started to feel
like typing through syrup. `meshsolve` is single-threaded, takes twenty to fifty
minutes of CPU for a normal scene, and cheerfully occupies whatever core it lands
on for all of it. Priya gave it a line of its own:

```
*	*	.*meshsolve.*	*	2	9	15
```

A nudge after two minutes, held down after twenty, buried after an hour. That
settled the syrup, and it is how every other line in the file was written too —
her predecessor's, the one for the screen savers, the catch-all at the top. Every
number in `/etc/and.priorities` on that machine was a nice level, because every
number in it was positive.

## The solve that wouldn't stop

What brought her back to the file, in September, was the other failure mode. A
`meshsolve` had been sitting at nice 15 since Saturday: no progress, no output,
just a core turning over on a degenerate mesh. Photogrammetry knew the shape of
it — a solve that has not converged inside an hour of CPU is never going to
converge — and what they wanted was for the thing to stop, because until it does
it is holding the floating licence that the rest of the group queues for.

Nice 15 does not achieve that. A job at nice 15 is a job that still exists,
still holds its licence, still holds the scratch directory open; all `and` has
done is make it slow. This is the case the priority database has a third column
for, and `and.priorities(5)` describes what to put in it in one sentence:
positive numbers and zero are interpreted as nice levels, negative numbers as
signals to be sent to the command. A level of 19 will almost stop a job; `-9`
will actually kill it, like `kill -9`.

The column is overloaded on purpose, and `and(8)` names the case it was built
for: *a certain web browser who shall remain unnamed tends to go berserk once in
a while*, and renicing is not a remedy for a job that has stopped making
progress. Before that number could go negative, reaping a runaway meant a second
tool standing next to the daemon — a cron job with `ps`, an `awk` field or two, a
`kill`, and a comment explaining why `ps` output was being parsed at all. Putting
the signal in the same column as the nice level means one file describes the
whole policy: how far a job sinks, and the point at which it stops sinking and
goes away.

Having never written a negative number into that file, Priya went to the manual
page for a worked example, and there is exactly one:

```
# Netscape sometimes goes berserk, we must stop it
* * netscape * 4 -9 -9
```

She wanted the same idea one step later — sink at two minutes, sink further at
twenty, and at an hour, when a solve is past saving, kill it. She went to her
line and typed the minus in.

```
*	*	.*meshsolve.*	*	2	-9	15
```

Then `kill -HUP` on the daemon, because `and` reloads the config and the priority
database on HUP rather than making you restart it, and back to the ticket queue.
Syslog recorded the one line it always records at that point:

```
and[1184]: Re-reading configuration and priority database...
```

## What the wrapper saw

Thursday morning there were two tickets, both from photogrammetry, both saying
the same thing: a `meshsolve` run had stopped in the night. Neither had failed,
in the sense of writing an error; the wrapper script that submits them had logged
what wrappers log:

```
meshsolve exited with status 137
```

Priya knows what 137 is the way everyone eventually knows what 137 is: 128 plus
9, a shell reporting a process killed by signal 9. So the first theory was the
OOM killer, which is where everybody's first theory goes, and `dmesg` had nothing
in it at all. No out-of-memory report, no segfault, no oops. `bench03` had 40 GB
free.

The second theory was that somebody was reaping jobs by hand, and that one lasted
until she worked out how long the two runs had lived. Both had burned a little
over twenty minutes of CPU before they stopped — which is a perfectly ordinary
length for a solve, and nowhere near the hour at which she had meant anything to
happen.

## The dry run

`and` has a test mode, and the reason to reach for it here is that it answers the
question directly rather than by inference. With `-t` the daemon does everything
it normally does — reads the config, walks the process table, works out the
verdict for every process — and then declines to carry any of it out, logging
what it would have done instead. `-s` sends that to stdout rather than the
`./debug.and` file it otherwise writes, and each `-v` raises the verbosity by one.

```
# and -t -s -vv
```

Two `-v` is the level at which `and` dumps the priority database it has just
parsed, as a table, before it looks at a single process. Not the text of the file
— the table it built out of the file:

```
UID:   GID:   Command               Parent:                             NLs:
   -1     -1  *                     *                    (parent)        4, 8,12
   -1     -1  .*meshsolve.*         *                    (parent)        2,-9,15
```

Every wildcard comes out of the parser as `-1`, so the table is a field of minus
signs before you reach the levels at all. Hers was in the levels, and it was in
the second of the three. She had put it where the example had it. In the example
the machine is a desktop and the job is a web browser that has gone berserk, so
the kill lands at the twenty-minute mark and the third column is a formality; in
hers, twenty minutes is the middle of an ordinary afternoon's work.

A minute later the same run reported what it would have done about the solve
currently on the box:

```
would kill -9 20514 (meshsolve)
```

Nothing in the file was malformed. `and` reads the three levels with a plain
`%i` and does not range-check them, because there is no range to check against:
in a column that means a nice level when it is positive and a signal number when
it is negative, `-9` is exactly as legal as `9`, and a line that says one when
you meant the other is a line the parser has no opinion about. Internally it is
the same branch either way — above zero the number goes to `setpriority`, below
zero it goes to `kill` with the sign flipped off — and which branch a job takes
is decided three columns deep in a text file.

The daemon had said nothing about it either, because in normal operating mode the
`kill` message is written at verbosity one and the verbosity starts at zero. It
had killed two jobs and told syslog nothing. Priya put `DAEMON_OPTS="-v"` in
`/etc/default/and` before she touched anything else.

Then she moved the minus one column to the right, which is where she had meant
to put it, and the digits stayed where they were: `9` in the middle, `15` at the
end, and the minus now in front of the 15. That is signal 15 rather than signal
9 — a `TERM` instead of a `KILL` — and she left it that way on purpose, because
a solve being stopped for spinning should get the chance to drop its licence on
the way out rather than leave the licence server to time it out.

```
# Positive numbers are nice levels. Negative numbers are signals.
# -15 is TERM, and it fires at lv3time: an hour of CPU. See and.priorities(5).
*	*	.*meshsolve.*	*	2	9	-15
```

`kill -HUP 1184`, and the same dry run again to read the table back:

```
   -1     -1  .*meshsolve.*         *                    (parent)        2, 9,-15
```

Both jobs were resubmitted that afternoon and both finished, the later of them at
16:51. The next one to wedge went eleven days later, on a Monday, and this time
the daemon wrote it down:

```
and[1184]: kill -15 4471 (meshsolve)
```
