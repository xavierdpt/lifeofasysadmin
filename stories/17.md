# Four Bytes Down the Optical Cable

*Theme: Pushback*

The dub room at Halliwell Transfer has one job on a Tuesday afternoon: take a
finished documentary mix off the playout box and lay it onto a reference disc for
the client's lawyer, who still wants a disc. The playout box is a Linux machine
with an optical output. The recorder is a consumer CD deck from the early 2000s,
kept alive because the lawyer's habits have outlived three formats.

At half past one, Wren came and found Aubrey at the other end of the building and
said the deck wouldn't arm. Not a level problem. The deck's input indicator was
locked to 48 kHz, the meters were moving, the monitors in the dub room were
playing the mix. Press record and the display refused the copy.

Aubrey started where the audio would have failed, and the audio hadn't.

    $ aplay -D iec958:CARD=Digi -f dat /srv/masters/hw2/reel3.wav

Played through, clean, meters and all. So the optical link was carrying audio and
the deck was declining it for a reason that had nothing to do with whether the
audio was any good.

    $ amixer -c1 contents | grep -A2 "IEC958 Playback Default"
    numid=9,iface=PCM,name='IEC958 Playback Default'
      ; type=IEC958,access=rw------,values=1
      : values=[AES0=0x00 AES1=0x00 AES2=0x00 AES3=0x02]

That is the whole of what the mixer tools will tell you. The element type is
IEC958, `amixer` prints the four bytes as hex and stops; `alsamixer` doesn't even
show them, because the control it draws is the switch, and what it does to the
name on its way to the screen is swap the string `IEC958` for `S/PDIF`, so the
label matches what is printed on the back of the machine. Four bytes, read-write, unexplained.

Those four bytes are channel status. It rides in the same optical stream as the
audio, one bit per sample, assembled at the far end into a block of 192 bits, of
which ALSA hands you the first four bytes as a single control element. What it
carries is a description of the material: what it is, what rate it claims, where
it came from, whether it may be copied. The receiver is entitled to act on that,
and a consumer recorder from 2003 very much does. Which is why `alsa-utils` ships
a twenty-year-old program whose entire subject is those four bytes:

    $ iecset -c1
    Mode: consumer
    Data: audio
    Rate: 48000 Hz
    Copyright: protected
    Emphasis: none
    Category: general
    Original: 1st generation
    Clock: 1000 ppm

Nothing about the machine had changed. The bits had been saying that since the
card came up. The deck was reading line four and doing what SCMS decks do.

The fix for the session took one command.

    $ iecset -c1 cop off
    ...
    Copyright: permitted
    ...

`iecset` inverts that one for you, which is worth knowing before you go editing
the byte by hand: the bit in the stream is *not copyright*, AES0 bit 2, zero for
copyrighted and one for not. Turning copyright "off" sets it. AES0 went from 0x00
to 0x04, the deck armed, and the disc was cut by half past three.

The change request arrived the next morning, from Wren, sensibly enough: put it
in the playbook. Every transfer host, at boot, so nobody loses an afternoon to
this again.

Aubrey said no, and took a minute over why, because "it's one bit" was the whole
appeal of the request.

The bit is not a setting on the playout box. It is a sentence the playout box
says about whatever it is playing, to whatever is listening, for as long as it
stays set. Set it at boot and it is set for the next job too, and the job after
that, and roughly a third of the material that passes through the dub room is
licensed footage that Halliwell has permission to transfer and no right to
declare copy-free at the far end of an optical cable. The deck can't tell the
difference between the mix they made and a rights-holder's master. The bits are
the only thing telling it, and the proposal was to make them tell it the same
thing every time.

The second half of the objection was narrower and did more work in the room.
"Copyright" is not one flag. SCMS reads it together with the original/generation
bit — `Original:` in that dump, AES1 bit 7 — and the ALSA header that defines it
carries the note that its meaning depends on the category code, which was still
`general`, because nobody had set that either. A boot-time `cop off` on every host
would leave three fields nobody had thought about being read by decks nobody had
tested against.

Wren listened to all of it and said: the archive is first-party, the fixtures are
first-party, the licensed material never goes out on disc, and the schedule has
four transfers on Thursday. Do it.

Which was Wren's call to make. Aubrey wrote it down instead of winning it.

The playbook got the line, scoped to the one host and the one card, with a comment
naming who asked, on what date, and the sentence about licensed material that the
comment does not have the power to enforce. The transfer script got two lines
around every job:

    iecset -c${CARD} -x | tee -a /var/log/transfer/$(date +%F).log
    iecset -c${CARD} cop off -x | tee -a /var/log/transfer/$(date +%F).log

`-x` prints the status as `AES0=0x04,AES1=0x00,AES2=0x00,AES3=0x02` — the same
form the `iec958` PCM plugin takes as its argument, which means the log line is
also a way to reproduce the state later. Thursday's log opens with the before and
after for each of the four transfers, and the operator's initials underneath.

The first of them started at 09:20. The deck armed on the first press.
