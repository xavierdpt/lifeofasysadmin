# A smoke detector tuned to the fire alarm

*Theme: Blue team and detection engineering*

The rule was three weeks old and I was proud of it, which should have been the
first warning.

We'd had a scare in the finance VLAN — a laptop whose ARP table briefly listed
the gateway at a MAC that belonged to nothing we owned, seen once, gone by the
time anyone looked. Probably a misconfigured print appliance. Probably. But
"probably" is not a word you get to keep using after the second time, so I spent
a sprint building the thing that would catch it if it ever came back with intent.

The detection was honest work. `arpwatch` on a SPAN port off the core switch,
feeding a parser that kept a table of who-claims-to-be-whom. An ARP reply whose
sender IP already mapped to a different MAC in the last hour raised a `flip flop`
and paged the on-call. On top of that a Zeek script counted gratuitous ARP
broadcasts per source and screamed if any host sent more than a handful a minute,
because that is what the tutorials tell you a poisoning tool looks like: a host
shouting "I am the gateway" to the whole segment, over and over, loud enough to
win the race against the real gateway's replies.

It fired on the lab rig every time I tested it with a hand-rolled Python script.
Green across the board. I wrote it up, closed the ticket, felt good.

Then, because the person who reviews your own work should occasionally be someone
who wants it to fail, I decided to attack it with something I hadn't written
myself. There is a little tool in the universe repos called `4g8` — Darren Bounds
wrote it in 2002 — that exists for exactly one reason, and the reason is worth
saying plainly because it is the whole shape of this story.

Once upon a time a network was a hub, and a hub was a party line: every frame
reached every port, and to read your neighbour's traffic you put your card in
promiscuous mode and listened. Switches killed that. A switch learns which MAC
lives behind which port and forwards a frame only there, so the attacker on the
next desk sees only their own traffic and broadcasts. Passive sniffing
just stops working. `4g8` is one of the answers to "how do I get back the view
the switch took away." It poisons one host into believing *you* are the gateway,
captures the traffic that host sends upstream — and then, and this is the part
that matters, re-injects each packet toward the real gateway so the conversation
keeps flowing. The man page is blunt about the cost: capture "at the expense of a
slight increase in latency to that third party host." Not an outage. A tax. If
you skipped the re-injection you'd have a denial of service and a very angry
user; the forwarding is the entire difference between a wiretap and a brick.

So I set it loose in the lab against a victim I owned:

```
# 4g8 -i eth0 -g 10.20.0.1 -G 00:1b:21:aa:bb:01 -s 10.20.4.37 -S 00:1b:21:44:0c:25
GW: (ip src host 10.20.4.37 and ether dst 00:15:5d:33:10:07) or (ip dst host 10.20.4.37 and ether dst 00:15:5d:33:10:07)
4g8: Lets see what 10.20.4.37 is up to. (Device: eth0)
```

`10.20.4.37` kept browsing. Its ping to the gateway never so much as blipped. And
my detection stayed dark.

That was the moment the sprint's worth of pride turned to something colder, and I
went and read what `4g8` actually puts on the wire instead of what I'd assumed a
poisoner does.

Two assumptions, both wrong. The first: I'd built the loud detector for a loud
attack — the broadcast flood. `4g8` doesn't broadcast. It sends its forged ARP
replies as *unicast*, one frame addressed straight to the victim's real MAC
telling it the gateway lives at my card, and a second addressed straight to the
gateway telling it the victim lives at my card. Two endpoints, two frames, nobody
else on the segment addressed at all. My Zeek counter was watching the town
square for someone shouting; `4g8` was slipping two notes under two specific
doors. And because those unicast frames were destined for ports that weren't
mine, the SPAN session — which mirrors the traffic the switch *chooses* to
forward — carried them to `arpwatch` only by luck of how the mirror was scoped.
On the segment where the poisoning happened, the only two machines that reliably
saw those replies were the two being lied to.

The second assumption was about tempo. I'd imagined a frantic loop racing the
gateway. `4g8` sets an interval timer — first refresh a second in, then one every
two seconds after that — just often enough to keep the forged entries warm before
the OS ages them out. Two ARP replies every two seconds is not a flood. It
wouldn't clear the per-minute threshold I'd picked if it ran all day.

And there was a signature sitting in plain sight that I hadn't thought to look
for, because I'd been counting volume instead of reading content. The replies
`4g8` forges aren't well-formed gratuitous ARPs. A real gratuitous ARP names
itself in both the sender and target fields. These name the impersonated gateway
as sender and leave the target protocol address as `0.0.0.0` with an all-zero
target hardware address — a reply to a question no one asked, pointing at nobody.
Unsolicited ARP replies are common enough from chatty appliances that you can't
page on them alone, but *reply, unsolicited, target IP `0.0.0.0`, sender IP is a
gateway* is a much narrower thing, and it was true of every frame the tool sent.

The last blind spot was the one I'd designed in without noticing. My mental
model of "poisoned" was "broken" — I half-expected the real defence to be a
connectivity alert, the victim's traffic dropping when its packets went to the
wrong MAC. But re-injection is the tool's whole reason for existing. The victim
never loses the gateway. The synthetic "host down" tell I'd been quietly relying
on never trips, because from every angle that a monitoring system watches,
`10.20.4.37` is having a completely normal day.

I rebuilt the detection around what the tool does rather than what the folklore
says an attacker looks like. Alert on any ARP *reply* not preceded by a matching
request within a short window; weight it up hard when the target protocol address
is `0.0.0.0` or the sender IP is a known gateway with an unknown MAC; and bind
the gateway's MAC statically on the switches that support it so the forged reply
has nothing to overwrite in the first place. I lowered the flood threshold too,
but I stopped treating it as the primary signal. A tool that only needs to send
two quiet packets every two seconds is never going to be caught by a rule that
waits for noise.

When I closed the ticket the second time I didn't feel proud. I felt like someone
who had almost shipped a smoke detector tuned to the sound of a fire alarm.
