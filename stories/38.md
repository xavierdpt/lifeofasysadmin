# The License Inside the License

*Theme: Supply chain*

The appliance shipped to a county sheriff's office in October, and the contract that
paid for it had a clause nobody on the engineering side had read closely until legal
forwarded it on a Tuesday: a full open-source attestation, every third-party component
named, every license text attached, due before the next unit left the warehouse. Noor
had built the imaging pipeline that let the box mount a seized Mac's drive read-only
and walk its APFS volumes without touching them, and `apfsprogs` was the part of that
pipeline she hadn't written herself — `apfsck`, to validate a volume before anything
touched it, the checker Ernesto Fernández built to test his own out-of-tree kernel
module rather than something designed to ship inside someone else's product.

The SPDX scanner had already run against the build and come back with one line for it:
`apfsprogs: GPL-2.0-only`. That matched the `LICENSE` file at the top of the source
tree, word for word the text of GPL-2. The attestation needed a signature with her
name on it, though, not the scanner's, so she went to `debian/copyright` instead —
the file that lists every path with its own license, rather than one for the whole
tree.

Most of the entries were exactly what the scanner had found: `Files: *`, GPL-2, Ernesto
Fernández's name on all of it. Two entries weren't. `lib/checksum.c` carried Gary S.
Brown's 1986 notice and a permissive one-paragraph grant instead of GPL-2. And
`lib/unicode.c` carried a block that ran to sixty lines — copyright 1991 through 2018,
Unicode, Inc., with a notice-and-permission license that opened with "NOTICE TO USER:
Carefully read the following legal agreement" and closed with a clause reserving the
Unicode name from use in advertising. Nothing in `LICENSE` or the top-of-tree
`GPL-2-only` stanza said anything about either of those two files.

She opened `lib/unicode.c` expecting a short wrapper around a system call. It ran to
thirty-two hundred lines. Past the UTF-8 decoding routines near the top, the rest of
the file was five arrays — `nfd_trie`, `nfd_values`, `cf_trie`, `cf_values`,
`ccc_trie` — thousands of hex entries, a compiled-in trie for Unicode normalization
and case folding with no plausible author but Unicode, Inc.'s own published data
tables. This wasn't a file that happened to sit under a stricter license by
accident of where someone pasted it from: `debian/copyright` named Unicode, Inc. as
the copyright holder for it, not Fernández, the same way it named Gary Brown for
`checksum.c`.

The attestation needed more than a list, though — it needed her to say whether the
component could ship without that file, since swapping out one dependency's license
for a friendlier one is sometimes the easy fix. She searched for where the tables
got used and found a single call site in `apfsck/key.c`: `dentry_hash()`, which walks
a filename through `normalize_next()` with case folding turned on or off depending on
`apfs_is_case_insensitive()`, and feeds the normalized, case-folded result into
`crc32c()` to produce the key a directory b-tree record is actually filed and looked
up under. `apfsck/super.c` confirmed which volumes that path has to run for:
`APFS_INCOMPAT_CASE_INSENSITIVE` and `APFS_INCOMPAT_NORMALIZATION_INSENSITIVE` are
both incompatible-feature flags: elsewhere in the same file, a flag `apfsck` doesn't
recognize is grounds to stop checking the volume outright, not just note it and move
on. `read_dir_rec_key()` recomputes that hash for every hashed directory
record on disk and compares it to the one stored in the key; get the normalization
wrong by a single codepoint and `apfsck` prints "filename hash is corrupted." The
Unicode tables weren't incidental to the tool; they were the only reason it could
open a drive that happened to set either flag, and nothing in the imaging pipeline
chose which drives came in for checking.

She wrote up the finding as three license
entries instead of one — GPL-2 for the bulk of the tree, the unrestricted grant for
`checksum.c`, the Unicode notice for `unicode.c` — attached the full sixty-line
Unicode text to the attestation packet rather than summarizing it, and flagged the
scanner's single-line result to the tooling team, so the next component it graded
would get checked against `debian/copyright` too, not just the license file sitting
at the top of the tree. The attestation went out Thursday afternoon, two files later
than the SPDX report had said it needed to be.
