# Two Files for the Central Store

*Theme: Onboarding and mentoring*

Ines Carvalho had been writing Group Policy at Harrowgate Freight since the domain
was still called `HGF-NT`. When the forty Ubuntu laptops used by the route-planning
team were moved from "Tomasz's problem" to "the desktop team's problem", they became
hers, and she started the way she would with any new batch of machines: she checked
what the domain was already doing to them.

The first thing she found was the logon notice. Every Windows workstation at
Harrowgate showed the same box before the password prompt: *Harrowgate Freight —
Authorised use only. Activity on this system is logged.* Legal had asked for it after
an employment tribunal in 2014, and it had lived ever since in a GPO called
*Workstation Logon Notice*, linked at the root of the domain so nothing could escape
it.

The Ubuntu laptops escaped it. She logged out of one and looked at the login screen:
a clock, a user list, a background. No notice.

She walked over to Tomasz Wrona's desk with the laptop under her arm.

"Your laptops don't do Group Policy."

"They do some." He took the laptop, logged in with his own domain account and
opened a terminal. "They're joined with SSSD. That's what lets you log in as
`ines@harrowgate.lan` in the first place, and SSSD does read a few security settings
out of GPOs, the logon rights mostly. Everything else is `adsys`. Watch."

```
$ adsysctl policy applied
Policies from machine configuration:
- Workstation Logon Notice ({7E1C04B2-5A9D-4F3E-B7A1-2D9C8E6F0A13})
- Default Domain Policy ({31B2F340-016D-11D2-945F-00C04FB984F9})

Policies from user configuration:
- Default Domain Policy ({31B2F340-016D-11D2-945F-00C04FB984F9})
```

Ines leaned in. "That's my GPO."

"That's your GPO. The machine found it, worked out it was in scope and downloaded
it. It applied too, as far as adsys is concerned. Now ask it what it applied."

```
$ adsysctl policy applied --details
Policies from machine configuration:
- Workstation Logon Notice ({7E1C04B2-5A9D-4F3E-B7A1-2D9C8E6F0A13})
- Default Domain Policy ({31B2F340-016D-11D2-945F-00C04FB984F9})

Policies from user configuration:
- Default Domain Policy ({31B2F340-016D-11D2-945F-00C04FB984F9})
```

The output looked the same. With `--details`, each GPO should have had its settings
listed under it, the key and the value that had landed on the machine. Neither GPO
had any.

"So it read it and did nothing with it."

"It read *part* of it. Look at what it actually pulled down." adsys keeps a copy of
every GPO it fetches from SYSVOL under `/var/cache/adsys/sysvol`, laid out the same
way as the share.

```
$ sudo find '/var/cache/adsys/sysvol/Policies/{7E1C04B2-5A9D-4F3E-B7A1-2D9C8E6F0A13}' -type f
/var/cache/adsys/sysvol/Policies/{7E1C04B2-5A9D-4F3E-B7A1-2D9C8E6F0A13}/GPT.INI
/var/cache/adsys/sysvol/Policies/{7E1C04B2-5A9D-4F3E-B7A1-2D9C8E6F0A13}/Machine/Microsoft/Windows NT/SecEdit/GptTmpl.inf
```

Ines knew that file. She had set the notice where everyone sets it, under *Computer
Configuration › Windows Settings › Security Settings › Local Policies › Security
Options*, the two *Interactive logon: Message…* entries, and the Security Settings
extension stores those in the security template, not in `Registry.pol`. Tomasz ran
it through `iconv`, because the file is UTF-16 and `cat` turns it into spaced-out
noise:

```
$ sudo iconv -f utf-16 -t utf-8 '/var/cache/adsys/sysvol/Policies/{7E1C04B2-5A9D-4F3E-B7A1-2D9C8E6F0A13}/Machine/Microsoft/Windows NT/SecEdit/GptTmpl.inf'
[Unicode]
Unicode=yes
[Version]
signature="$CHICAGO$"
Revision=1
[Registry Values]
MACHINE\Software\Microsoft\Windows\CurrentVersion\Policies\System\LegalNoticeCaption=1,"Harrowgate Freight"
MACHINE\Software\Microsoft\Windows\CurrentVersion\Policies\System\LegalNoticeText=7,Authorised use only. Activity on this system is logged.
```

"There's your notice, on the laptop, on disk," Tomasz said. "adsys doesn't read that
file. It opens `Registry.pol` under `Machine` or `User`, and in there it only looks
at keys under `Software\Policies\Ubuntu`. Anything else, it skips."

He did not expect her to take that on trust, so he opened the adsys source, which he
kept around for exactly this kind of argument, and scrolled to the loop in
`internal/ad/ad.go` that decodes `Registry.pol`:

```go
// Only consider supported policies for this distro
if !strings.HasPrefix(pol.Key, keyFilterPrefix) {
	continue
}
```

`keyFilterPrefix` was built a few lines up from `Software/Policies` and the distro
name, `Ubuntu`.

"Why not translate them?" Ines asked. "LegalNoticeText is LegalNoticeText. The
laptop has a login screen, it could show it."

"This one would be easy. Most wouldn't. What does 'Prevent changing desktop
background' mean on GNOME? Which key, and is it the same key on every release?
GNOME settings come and go between versions. The adsys docs give three reasons
for keeping Ubuntu in its own namespace: namespaces clash, Windows and Linux paths use
different slashes, and the admin has to be able to see which settings a given client
version actually supports. If adsys guessed at your Windows settings, you'd have to
test every one on a laptop to find out what the guess was. With its own namespace,
Group Policy Management Editor shows you exactly what it can do."

"Group Policy Management Editor doesn't show me anything Ubuntu."

"Because you don't have the templates yet." He typed:

```
$ cd /tmp && adsysctl policy admx lts-only && ls
Ubuntu.adml  Ubuntu.admx
```

"Those two files go in your Central Store. You know the drill better than I do."

She did. `Ubuntu.admx` went into
`\\harrowgate.lan\SYSVOL\harrowgate.lan\Policies\PolicyDefinitions`, next to the
Windows templates that had been there since 2016, and `Ubuntu.adml` into the `en-US`
folder under it. `lts-only` meant the templates listed the LTS releases, 20.04, 22.04
and 24.04, and not the interim ones. Tomasz pointed out that he had generated them
from the adsys on a 24.04 laptop on purpose: the upstream repository has copies too,
but only the templates `adsysctl` produces are guaranteed to match what the installed
client understands.

Back at her own desk, she created a new GPO, *Ubuntu Logon Notice*, and linked it to
the `Workstations\Linux` OU where the laptops' computer objects lived. In the editor,
under *Computer Configuration › Policies › Administrative Templates*, there was now
an *Ubuntu* folder. *Login Screen › Interface* held the two settings she wanted:
*Enable showing the banner message* and *Banner message text*. The explanation pane
on the second one said what it did underneath:

```
- Type: dconf
- Key: /org/gnome/login-screen/banner-message-text
- Default: ''

Note: default system value is used for "Not Configured" and enforced if "Disabled".

Supported on Ubuntu 20.04, 22.04, 24.04.
```

Each setting had a value field for all releases and, under it, an *Override*
checkbox and field for each release. She filled in the top one on both, ticked
*Enabled* on both, and walked back over.

"On Windows I'd `gpupdate /force`."

"`adsysctl update -m`. The `-m` is the machine half. Without it, it refreshes your
user policy."

```
$ adsysctl update -m
$
```

"Silence is success," Tomasz said, and logged out. This time the login screen
showed the notice: *Authorised use only. Activity on this system is logged.* He
logged in again and asked the same question as before.

```
$ adsysctl policy applied --details
Policies from machine configuration:
- Ubuntu Logon Notice ({C9A83F51-0E27-4B6D-9D14-6A5F2B7E3C08})
    - gdm:
        - dconf/org/gnome/login-screen/banner-message-enable: true
        - dconf/org/gnome/login-screen/banner-message-text: Authorised use only. Activity on this system is logged.
- Workstation Logon Notice ({7E1C04B2-5A9D-4F3E-B7A1-2D9C8E6F0A13})
- Default Domain Policy ({31B2F340-016D-11D2-945F-00C04FB984F9})

Policies from user configuration:
- Default Domain Policy ({31B2F340-016D-11D2-945F-00C04FB984F9})
```

The `gdm:` line mattered. The login screen runs as its own user, so adsys writes
login-screen settings into a separate dconf database for it rather than the one the
desktop session reads. Tomasz showed her the two files it had produced:

```
$ cat /etc/dconf/db/gdm.d/adsys
[org/gnome/login-screen]
banner-message-enable=true
banner-message-text='Authorised use only. Activity on this system is logged.'
$ cat /etc/dconf/db/gdm.d/locks/adsys
/org/gnome/login-screen/banner-message-enable
/org/gnome/login-screen/banner-message-text
```

"Before adsys, this is what I did," he said. "I wrote those two files by hand,
shipped them with a script I ran from my laptop, and ran `dconf update`. It worked,
but it was a second policy system that only I knew about, targeted by a list of
hostnames in a text file instead of your OUs. Legal thought the notice was
enforced everywhere because your GPO said so. For forty machines it wasn't, and
nobody on your team could have found that out from Group Policy Management."

"And if somebody with sudo deletes the lock file?"

"Then the banner goes until the next refresh. adsys applies machine policy at boot,
user policy at login, and there's a timer for everything in between." He checked it:

```
$ systemctl cat adsys-gpo-refresh.timer | grep -A2 '^\[Timer'
[Timer]
OnBootSec=90min
OnUnitActiveSec=90min
```

"Ninety minutes after boot, then every ninety minutes. Same interval as your
background refresh on Windows, without the random offset. The lock stops any other
dconf database from overriding the value. A local admin can still edit the files,
but the next refresh puts them back."

Ines had one more question, and it was the one that showed she had been listening.
"The note said *enforced if Disabled*. On Windows, Disabled means off."

"Here it's closer to 'locked at the default'. *Not Configured* leaves the key alone
and the laptop's own settings win. *Enabled* writes your value and locks it.
*Disabled* writes no value and locks the key anyway, so the laptop is stuck on
whatever the system default is. For the banner that happens to look like 'off',
because the default is `false`. For something like the wallpaper, it means the user
can't change it and gets the stock one."

She wrote that down. Then she went back to her desk and did what she would have done
with any new estate: she opened the Default Domain Policy and every GPO linked above
`Workstations\Linux` and checked each Windows setting against the *Ubuntu* tree to
see which ones had a counterpart. Most of the desktop ones did, under *Desktop* and
*Login Screen*. The screen-lock timeout did not: the 24.04 templates had the
screensaver picture and a switch to disable the lock screen, but no idle delay.

She sent Tomasz the list with that line highlighted. *This one stays in your script
for now. Everything above it is mine.*
