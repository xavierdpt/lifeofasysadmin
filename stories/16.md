# Three Fifteen in Room B-14

*Theme: Heisenbug*

The ticket from the AV team said the same thing every fortnight, in slightly different
words: *B-14 recorder failed to start, no audio device, please reset the sound.* The
recorder in B-14 was a small rack PC that took the lectern microphone off the desk mixer
and wrote an hour of lecture to the capture share. When it failed, it failed at the top
of the hour with a line in its log about the capture device being busy, and by the time
anyone from infrastructure got to a terminal, it was not busy at all.

Priya inherited the ticket on her third week. The runbook entry was four words long —
"run `alsa force-reload`, then retry" — and it worked. It always worked. That was the
part she could not leave alone.

`/sbin/alsa` comes from the `alsa-base` package, which is one of the odder survivals in
the archive: a source package last touched upstream in the ALSA 1.0.25 era, still
shipping a handful of modprobe fragments and this one shell script. The script has six
verbs — `unload`, `reload`, `force-unload`, `force-reload`, `suspend`, `resume` — and its
own header explains that it is no longer run at boot or shutdown. It sits there for the
moment a human needs the sound stack taken down and put back.

She read what `force-reload` actually did before she ran it again, and that was where the
fortnight of guessing ended.

## What the force is for

Taking a sound card's modules out is easy to describe and annoying to do. `modprobe -r
snd_hda_intel` will tell you the module is in use, and it is right: something has a file
descriptor open on `/dev/snd/pcmC0D0c`, and until that descriptor is closed the module
refcount stays above zero. Before the script existed, the manual version of this was a
loop — `fuser -v /dev/snd/*`, read the PIDs, kill them, try `modprobe -r` again, discover
that `snd_hda_intel` came out but `snd_pcm` is still held by a second process you missed,
kill that one too. Then, having finally got the card out, you had to remember which dozen
`snd-*` modules you had removed so you could put them back in an order that worked.

`force-unload` is that loop written down once. It scans `/proc/[0-9]*/fd/*`, following
each link and keeping the ones that point into `/dev/snd/pcm`, which gives it the exact
set of processes holding a PCM device. It sends them `SIGTERM`, sleeps a second, scans
again, and repeats up to four times before falling back to `SIGKILL`. Then it unloads
every loaded `snd-*` module — and, crucially, writes their names into
`/var/run/alsa/modules-removed` first, so that the `load_unloaded_modules` half of
`reload` has something to read back and `modprobe` in turn. If it cannot create
`/var/run/alsa`, it says so in as many words:

```
/sbin/alsa: Warning: Not keeping list of removed modules because /var/run/alsa is absent.
It will not be possible automatically to reload these modules.
```

Which is the honest version of what the sysadmin doing this by hand always risked.

So `force-reload` fixed B-14 every time. It fixed it by killing whatever held the device
and starting the stack clean, and it had been fixing it that way for however many
fortnights, which meant that every single time somebody investigated the problem, the
investigation destroyed the only evidence that would have identified it. The process
holding the capture device was killed, unnamed, by the fix, and the report came back
*resolved, sound reset.*

## The quiet verb

The thing Priya noticed reading the script is that plain `unload` does not kill anything.
It does the same scan of `/proc/*/fd`, but where `force_unload_modules` hands the list to
the kill loop, `unload_modules` only warns about it, runs `modprobe -r` on each module,
lets every failure fall on the floor, and then reports what is still loaded and exits
non-zero. It is the same diagnostic machinery with the violence removed: it tells you who
is holding the card, and leaves them holding it.

It also prints the offenders with their command names — `ps --no-headers -o "%p %c"`,
reformatted to `PID(comm)` — which is the whole answer, if you can get it to run at a
moment when the problem is present.

That was the other half. Every attempt so far had been made by a person standing at the
lectern in B-14, or by a person on a phone with a person standing at the lectern. Priya
put the probe somewhere a person wasn't:

```
$ ssh b14-rec 'at 03:15' <<'EOF'
/sbin/alsa unload > /var/tmp/alsa-probe.txt 2>&1
EOF
```

At ten past nine the next morning:

```
/sbin/alsa: Warning: Processes using sound devices: 2211(mpv).
Unloading ALSA sound driver modules: snd-hda-codec-hdmi snd-hda-intel snd-hda-codec
 snd-hda-core snd-hwdep snd-pcm snd-timer snd (failed: modules still loaded:
 snd-hda-codec-hdmi snd-hda-intel snd-hda-codec snd-hda-core snd-hwdep snd-pcm
 snd-timer snd).
```

`mpv`. Nothing in the capture chain used `mpv`.

## Why the room had to be empty

B-14's screen showed the department's signage loop when the room was idle — timetable,
seminar posters, a silent video of the campus in autumn that was not, it turned out,
silent. The loop was started by the idle hook, and the hook launched `mpv` with defaults,
which meant `mpv` opened the default ALSA device on startup and kept it open for as long
as it played. That was harmless in itself. What was not harmless was that the recorder's
scheduled start and the idle loop could overlap: if a lecturer left ten minutes early and
the room went idle before the recorder was done setting up for the next slot, the signage
player had the card and the recorder did not.

And the hook was bound to idle. Walk into B-14, touch the panel, move a mouse, and the
loop stopped and released the device. Log in at the console to have a look and the loop
stopped and released the device. The bug was visible only from a room with nobody in it,
which is why it had never once been visible to anyone sent to look at it, and why the
only tool that had ever been pointed at it was the one that killed the witness.

Priya changed one line in the signage unit's `ExecStart` to pass `--ao=null`, restarted
it, and let the room sit. The 09:00 lecture recorded. She ran the `at` job once more the
following Tuesday out of suspicion; `/var/tmp/alsa-probe.txt` came back with the unload
succeeding and no warning line above it.

The runbook entry is longer now. It still has `alsa force-reload` in it, under a heading
that says *to get the room working again*, and above a second heading that says *to find
out why*, under which the command is `alsa unload` and the instruction is to run it from
somewhere else, with the door shut.
