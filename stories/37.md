# Twenty-Two Minutes on the Prepress Server

*Theme: Communication under pressure*

The lights in the newsroom went out at 21:47 on election night. For a second the only light came from the laptops. Then the emergency strip over the fire door came on, and from the cupboard behind the sports desk came the steady chirp of a UPS that had just lost its mains.

Margit, the editor, was at Ines's desk before the chirping had settled into a rhythm.

"The pages," she said. "Tell me the pages are fine."

The pages lived on the prepress server: the machine that took the finished layouts, turned them into plates-ready PDFs and pushed them over a VPN to the print plant forty kilometres away. The plant's press slot was 23:00. Eight of the twelve election pages were done. The front page and the three results pages were waiting on the last count from the town hall.

Ines's laptop was on battery and the office switch was on the same UPS as the server, so her SSH session on the prepress server was still open. The broadcast was already in it:

```
Broadcast message from root@prepress (somewhere) (Sun May 17 21:47:18 2026):

Power failure on UPS prepress. Running on batteries.
```

The events file had two lines, six seconds apart:

```
$ tail -n 2 /var/log/apcupsd.events
2026-05-17 21:47:12 +0200  Power failure.
2026-05-17 21:47:18 +0200  Running on UPS batteries.
```

The six seconds were `ONBATTERYDELAY 6` from the stock config. apcupsd raises `powerout` the moment the UPS reports it has left the mains and holds `onbattery`, the event that sends the wall message and the mail, until the outage has lasted that long, so a flicker in the grid does not page anybody.

```
$ apcaccess status
APC      : 001,036,0878
DATE     : 2026-05-17 21:47:51 +0200
HOSTNAME : prepress
VERSION  : 3.14.14 (31 May 2016) debian
UPSNAME  : prepress
CABLE    : USB Cable

UPSMODE  : Stand Alone
STARTTIME: 2026-05-02 06:12:40 +0200
MODEL    : Smart-UPS 1500
STATUS   : ONBATT

...

...
MANDATE  : 2021-02-03
BATTDATE : 2021-02-03
```

"Twenty-two minutes," Ines said, and stopped.

Margit had already turned halfway towards the newsroom. Twenty-two minutes was a number she could act on. It meant waiting for the count until 22:00 and sending all twelve pages together. She would plan the next quarter of an hour around it, the reporter at the town hall would plan around it, and so would the plant.

The number was the UPS's, not apcupsd's. `TIMELEFT` is the remaining runtime the UPS computes from a counter it maintains internally; apcupsd reads it off the USB link and prints it. `BATTDATE` was the same as `MANDATE`, the date of manufacture. Either nobody had changed the batteries in five years, or somebody had changed them without updating the date in the EEPROM. Ines had been at the paper for two of those years, had changed nothing and had never run a calibration on the unit. The apcupsd manual's chapter on recalibrating the UPS runtime says what that costs: on old batteries, the UPS "can report a runtime of 5 minutes remaining when in fact only one minute remains".

"Twenty-two is what the box says," she told Margit. "The batteries are at least five years old and I've never had the box measure them. I don't believe twenty-two. Give me two minutes and I'll tell you what I do believe."

Margit went off to phone the town hall.

Ines put a loop on the screen:

```
$ while sleep 30; do printf '%s  ' "$(date +%T)"; apcaccess -u -p TIMELEFT; done
21:48:24  21.4
21:48:54  20.8
21:49:24  20.1
21:49:54  19.5
21:50:24  18.8
```

In two and a half minutes of wall clock the estimate had lost more than three.

What she could say with confidence was what apcupsd would do, because that was in `/etc/apcupsd/apcupsd.conf` and in `action.c`, not in the battery. There were three shutdown conditions, and the first one to trip wins. `BATTERYLEVEL 5` would shut down when the charge the UPS reported fell to 5 percent. `MINUTES 3` would shut down when `TIMELEFT` fell to three. `TIMEOUT 0` was a plain stopwatch on time spent on battery, and at zero it was off; the config comment recommends zero on a Smart-UPS, and on an older dumb UPS a value below the runtime you know it has. Behind all three sat the UPS's own low-battery signal. When the UPS raises it, apcupsd shuts the machine down five seconds later, whatever the other numbers say. The manual's recalibration chapter relies on it: "regardless of the runtime remaining that is reported, the UPS will always correctly detect low batteries".

So the machine would not crash. It would shut itself down cleanly, possibly a long time before `TIMELEFT` reached three.

At 21:51 she went to find Margit, who was standing by the window with the phone at her ear.

"It's losing about a minute and a quarter for every minute that passes. If that keeps up, it reaches three minutes, which is where it shuts itself down, at about two minutes past ten. It could be earlier. The UPS can call a shutdown by itself and it won't warn me first. If you want certainty, the pages go at five to ten, with whatever's on them."

"The count won't be in by five to."

"Then I can buy you a few minutes, but it's going to look strange."

In the cupboard, the UPS fed three things besides the prepress server: the office switch, a retired file server still powered on, and a monitor. Ines powered off the monitor, shut down the old file server over SSH, and watched the loop.

```
21:52:54  16.1
21:53:24  15.4
21:53:54  21.9
21:54:24  21.5
```

She went back to the window before Margit could see that number on anyone's screen.

"It's going to say twenty-two again. That doesn't mean we're back where we started. I took load off, so the same battery lasts longer at the lower draw, and it's still the same old battery. My number is now about quarter past ten for the shutdown. The pages go at ten past at the latest, whatever state the front page is in."

"Ten past." Margit wrote it on the back of her hand.

The count came in at 22:01. The front page went through at 22:04, and the results pages at 22:06 and 22:07. The plant's FTP log confirmed all twelve at 22:08. Ines left the loop running, mostly because she wanted to see how far off the UPS was.

```
22:08:54  8.0
22:09:24  7.4
```

The events file had the rest:

```
2026-05-17 22:09:41 +0200  Battery power exhausted.
2026-05-17 22:09:41 +0200  Initiating system shutdown!
```

The display still read seven and a half minutes when the UPS raised its low-battery signal. The line apcupsd logs when `MINUTES` trips, "Reached remaining time percentage limit on batteries.", never appeared. The prepress server went down cleanly at 22:10. The mains came back at 22:31 and the paper was on the doorsteps at six.

---

On Tuesday two replacement battery packs arrived. Ines fitted them on Saturday morning, when the only people in the building were the cleaner and the sports desk.

The manual's recalibration chapter explains why the calibration step exists. The UPS's runtime estimate comes from a counter the UPS keeps of what its batteries can deliver. As the batteries age they hold less charge, and the counter can go on reflecting the capacity they had when new, so the estimate can sit above the truth for years without any sign. A calibration run drains the batteries under a real load down to about a quarter of their charge, and has the UPS recalibrate the counter from the discharge. The manual recommends doing it about once a year and no more than twice, because deep discharges shorten the batteries' life. She wanted one on the new batteries before she quoted anyone a number from them.

`apctest` cannot talk to the UPS while apcupsd has the USB port, so she stopped the daemon first:

```
# systemctl stop apcupsd
# apctest
...
1)  Test kill UPS power
2)  Perform self-test
3)  Read last self-test result
4)  View/Change battery date
5)  View manufacturing date
...
10) Perform battery calibration
11) Test alarm
 Q) Quit

Select function number: 4
```

She set the battery date to that morning's. Then she waited, because the calibration refuses to start unless the charge reads exactly 100 percent and the load is at least 10. She had turned the old file server back on to supply the load.

```
Select function number: 10

operation and reports the result when the process completes.

```

She took her hands off the keyboard, because pressing Enter would abort the run. Then she opened a mail to Margit and started a sentence she would be able to finish once the dots stopped: "At election-night load, the prepress server now has".
